There are some books that many people know about, but very few have actually read. A certain picture of the book spreads and if it implies limited relevance that is where the story stops for most people. I think Fukuyama's 'The End of History and the Last Man' (1992) is one of these books. It came to be known as a triumphalist and it would be typically assumed rather superficial, perhaps even propagandistic, book that declared the triumph of liberal democracy (LD) and the market economy (ME) over all other forms of social organisation and thereby the futility of any challenge to this twin principle. Since the financial crisis of 2008 things have turned upside down not just in developing countries, but in rich Western democracies as well, and there is a widespread feeling that liberal democracy is in a (perhaps terminal) crisis. The general political turbulence has been sometimes referred to as 'the end of the end of history'. Is this justified, by the metrics of the original end-of-history (EOH) thesis? 

The first thing I would note, reading the book in 2024, 32 years after its publication, is how surprised, even shocked I was by its quality and main assertions, in a positive sense. This is a highly sophisticated book, not at all the ideological caricature as it is widely known or rather imagined. Secondly, I find its main theses stand up remarkably well even today. I do not intend to provide a detailed reconstruction of the book, in fact I do not offer a reconstruction at all. Instead I would take what seems to me the *best possible reading* of the book's main tenets and examine to what extent they seem valid today. This reading might well not correspond to the author's original intentions, or their views on the matter today. In other words I will try to use the book as a toolbox to be used in the most relevant way in 2024, rather than to provide a piece-by-piece representation of its theses. The main theses that can be extracted from the book can be summarised in a few simple points. My aim to be be as clear and simple as possible, doing the opposite of the usual operation in politics/humanities/social sciences. 

# The Four Theses 

## Directional Progress

### Science and technology

The first thesis is that there is a clear directionality to human history since the emergence of agriculture. There is a secular increase in the complexity and size of human societies and in the general welfare of the average individual, at least in physical terms (arguably the only kind of welfare that can be straightforwardly defined).  
What is the mechanism of this long-term, directional trend? It is modern science and technology (S&T), coupled with a market system, both driven by natural desires of humans for comfort and physical welfare. Modern S&T as a set of consistent institutions and practices only emerges from approximately the 17th century, and it only transforms society fundamentally starting from the 19th, but in most of the world the 20th century. It is a mechanism that has memory, retaining its previous results and therefore it is cumulative. Knowledge on how nature works and how it can be changed and used builds up and therefore the process is not cyclic, but rather clearly upwards, although showing fluctuations on a smaller scale.  
Science-based modern technology offers things to satisfy human needs, so there is constant demand for more S&T and as societies get richer, the resources available for S&T grow, forming a positive feedback loop. Fukuyama calls this 'the Mechanism' that explains the directionality towards better and more understanding of the physical world, larger-scale technologies to change and use nature and mobilise ever larger amounts of energy and physical resources and to satisfy more needs of more humans, making them live longer, healthier etc. I think despite libraries full of books on the contradictions of how S&T actually develops, the plausiblity and the evidence base for this thesis is overwhelming, and it makes no sense to even try to quarrel with it. 

### Market superiority

The second thesis is what we could call Market Superiority, that is the superiority of market-based economic systems. This too is ultimately underpinned by human desires for material welfare. The economic system that is most capable of 'growing the pie', of remaining dynamic and providing people with the stuff they want, will prevail.  
Economic growth on a massive scale is in fact a [very recent phenomenon](https://mbkoltai.github.io/illusion-globalisation/): around 98% of the increase in estimated (yearly) global output from AD 1 up to today happened since 1900, and nearly 90% since 1950. Therefore the relevant evidence base for gauging the superiority of the market system is the last 100-150 years, although extending this back further would I believe lead to the same conclusions.  
In either case, no other economic systems has proved to be even nearly competitive with market based societies, whether the attempted alternatives were based on state planning or localism and a rejection of modern economic growth. Socialist planning was partially succesful in the first phase of heavy industrialisation, but such systems could not carry out the transition to a modern consumer society with its myriad needs and wants that no state agency can track. Systems based on (socialist) planning either collapsed under the double pressure of the capitalist world market and their own population's passive/active rejection (USSR, Eastern Europe) or transitioned to a market economy (China), or closed in on themselves and regressed into stagnation and misery (Cuba, North Korea).  
Besides the impossibility of planning at a certain level of consumer complexity, systems based on planning fail on a perhaps even more fundamental level. Since they are centralised and cannot allow spontaneous, decentralised initiatives in the economy (as they would break up the coherence of the macroeconomic plan) they have to suppress individudal initiative of the economic sort. But this removes one of main sources of motivation of doing anything really and therefore there will be a huge loss in work morale, creativity and innovation, leading to stagnation. Moreover, such systems need to actively clamp down on anyone who wants to break off from the plan and try their own business, leading unavoidably to violence and repression. This is undesirable in itself, obviously, but it also undermines such societies, because besides killing off initiative it creates resentment, dissatisfaction and leads to a general erosion of the willingness to work or build new things. Hence, stagnation, apathy and eventually collapse - or a restoration of a market system.  
I write this as someone who tried to deny this for a long time and avoid the question of how an alternative to the market system could work without dictatorship and/or stagnation and collapse. I eventually had to come to the conclusion that this positions is untenable, and the reason why adversaries of the market system (mostly socialists) cannot give a convincing (or indeed any) answer to the question of an alternative is that there is not one; or at any rate they or anyone else could not come up with one so far, which is the same.

Beyond a certain level of complexity, only decentralised markets and private ownership of assets (so control over production and profit is clear-cut) can make societies reliably tick by providing both the motivation to work and innovate and by responding to consumer demand flexibly. Similarly to the 'Mechanism' of S&T producing a directional historical process, the strong 'attractor' of the market system - that modern societies will gravitate towards - is I believe very hard to counter, and I see no point trying to argue against it.  
Localisms that try to decouple from the global market economy will be swept away even easier than attempts at general planning, indeed, no such large scale experiments existed in the last 100 years I believe, while state socialist systems managed to resist their fate for 40-70 years.  
Therefore, there will be a tendency towards market-based economic systems in modern societies, and markets will reappear even when they are suppressed from above.  
This is not to deny the brutality and the enormous problems that market dynamics creates, or to argue that modern societies need to be *exclusively* based on markets, which is very probably both impossible and undesirable. Regulation of markets and some level of socialisation of certain economic spheres are very probably necessary and desirable to sustain market economies.  
Yet, the simple principle of decentralised initiative, of letting people do their stuff, have their initiative has to obtain and in all likelihood has to be *the* basis of any dynamic modern society.  
This second thesis of Marker Superiority has stood the test of time remarkably well since 1992, definitely in the sense of a total lack of systemic alternative. World economic output has [almost tripled](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/NY.GDP.MKTP.PP.KD?end=2023&locations=1W-CN&start=1990) since 1990, with (economically) liberalised China and India expanding from less than 5% to 27% of the world economy by today. So by its own metric, the market has clearly triumphed: the increment in global GDP since 1990 from 60 to 165 trillion USD ([2021 PPP USD](https://data.worldbank.org/indicator/NY.GDP.MKTP.PP.KD?end=2023&locations=HU-PL-CZ-ES-1W&start=1990)) larger than the increment from AD 1 to 1990!

This success has created its own problems of course, some of which seem existential: climate change and resource depletion in the form of fossile energy, possibly water or some rare metals or other minerals. Exponential growth in physical terms [can clearly not be sustained](https://dothemath.ucsd.edu/2012/04/economist-meets-physicist/) - putting aside everything else, the Earth's atmosphere would boil in a few centuries from now. But it seems to me this is a rather 'orthogonal' problem to the philosophical-hitorical one of possible alternatives to a market system, and also one that is not (automatically) solved by socialist planning. At some point, in physical terms the economic system will need to reach steady state (or expand massively beyond this planet). Population growth might plateau already in a few decades, and after that per capita energy and resource use would need to level off as well. Is this possible in a market system? In general competition is probably conducive to growth, but it does not need to be growth in terms of population or physical output: potentially, technologies and regulations can be found so that the net result is no growth in energy/resource use, although there can be endless variations on the specific products and services produced. But the point would still stand that the system would need to allow for decentralised initiative, ie. it needs to be some type of market system.  
One counter-argument here could be that such measures would be more easily implemented in a planned economy, or perhaps even exclusively possible in such a system. This is however not clear: regulation and/or technology could also hold back further growth in resource use, while a planned economy would still come up against the problems described above. But this is in any case hypothetical, as I think essentially nobody seriously believes now that a transition to a non-market based society is in the cards, not to mention elimination of private ownership of assets. So while it might be true that ecological problems cannot be solved in a market system (though we do not know this), it still holds that this system is a strong attractor that it is difficult to see what to replace with or how to exit.

On a more political note, this thesis does not say what type of state will govern a market-based society, and it may very well be an *authoritarian capitalist* state, rather than a democratic or totalitarian one. In fact, Fukuyama notes multiple times that a 'market authoritarian' system (such as South Korea and Taiwan in their catch-up phase) is not only not necessarily inferior to a *democratic* capitalist state in terms of economic efficiency, but is likely to be even superior, under some circumstance at least, as long as it is not a kleptocratic state where the elite siphon off profits instead of reinvesting. So while the case for the inevitability of technological progress and the drive towards a market-based consumer society appears very strong, and this represents a certain closing of the historical horizon on a systemic level (ie. the end of history), it is less clear that there is an inexorable trend towards a liberal-democratic political order. This leads us to the third main thesis. 

## The Liberal-Democratic Endpoint

### The Struggle for Recognition

The third main thesis we could call the Liberal-Democratic Endpoint. This is different in nature from the first two and very probably the most problematic. The first two theses are supported by arguments based on fairly uncontroversial observations about the nature of humans (indeed any animal) seeking pleasure and avoiding pain, and a simple dynamic hypothesis (observation) that over time systems that can cater to this 'desiring part of the soul' (as Fukuyama calls it) better - modern S&T and market-based economic systems - will prevail and therefore will function as absorber states (attractors) that history will tend towards and once there, remain within their orbits, barring total collapse. 

The 'Liberal-Democratic Endpoint' is supported by a very different type of argument that Fukuyama names '*the struggle for recognition*'. His main sources here are Nietzsche and Hegel, not evolutionary theory or plausible observations about economic history, although it is also supported by a kind of fairly plausible stylised history similar to an evolutionary just-so story.  
It goes like this: besides the 'desiring part of the soul' (or brain/body, ontology does not enter into this) whose aim is pleasure and self-preservation, human nature has another fundamental, 'thymotic' part that desires recognition from other humans. The primordial form of gaining recognition is to risk one's life in battle and thereby become a master of others: the origin of warrior aristocracies. Those who failed to do that at the beginnings of history became slaves and were reduced to a life of self-preservation, but little or no dignity and recognition.  
However - contrary to the purely utilitarian vision of Anglo-Saxon liberalism - humans cannot be content with self-preservation only. The slave still yearned for dignity and recognition and found a channel for it: work. Through work it is slaves - not proud but idle aristocracies - that kept history moving. Still resenting their slavehood ideas arose - most importantly Christianity - upholding the possibility of freedom for slaves, if not in this world than in an afterlife.  
Eventually, the slave revolted and risked *their lives* - as once their masters did - in revolutions to gain freedom: thereby they finally gained recognition, and then created a society that is based on mutual recognition. This is the realm of freedom and its political expression is modern liberal democracy. Fukuyama argues that Anglo-Saxon liberalism is an excessively reductive reading of and justification for liberal democracy: there, it is a society of reciprocal agreement not to interfere with each other's lives and property, so that all can pursue their happiness. It is a society of mutually agreed self-preservation, or more negatively of mutual apathy. In this second reading based on the concept of recognition, liberal society is more than that, it is a mutual agreement among citizens to recognise each other.  
Since the need for recognition is a fundamental part of human nature, and since all other forms of societies - that are based in some way on violent oppression - fail to satisfy this need, therefore liberal-democratic society will be an attractor on the long term. Political systems depriving people from the recognition they desire - as slave societies, absolute monarchies and modern dictatorships do - will be undermined and will eventually collapse and start a movement towards one (ie. liberal-democratic society) where all citizens get recognition, in the form of a set of inalienable rights and freedoms, granted simply on the universal basis that they are human beings.  
This is then the reason why liberal-democratic society is an attractor and why although regressions from it are possible but its trancension is unlikely.

The rationale for liberal democracy is not primarily economic. In fact, democracy is likely to be a drag on economic efficiency as it diverts resources from investment to redistribution and ultimately consumption. The liberal-democratic principle would tend to stubbornly re-emerge and eventually assert itself for a reason autonomous from the cumulative process of technological-economic growth: the need for recognition.  
At the same time, economic development helps the movement towards liberal-democratic society: it provides people with better work - through which they can find expressions for their dignity - and with education so they can learn and refine ideas of freedom and dignity. Economic development requires more education and more skilled work and so lays the groundwork for the need for recognition to assert itself over time. Hence the correlation between a high level of economic development and democracy: while they have different root causes, the latter is helped by the first. 

### The problem of the method
There are many issues in this narrative and it is hard to know where to begin. First, there is the starting hypothesis itself of the need for recognition being fundamental to human nature. To what extent is this verifiable? Fukuyama's book is a philosophical treatise and his argument is not empirical, but is rather based on philosophical authority (Hegel, Kojeve and Nietzsche) and a - claimed - general plausibility of this argument. Perhaps evolutionary biology and/or experimental psychology could support this argument more broadly and could even show that it is (or was) in fact an optimal strategy in an evolutionary sense as well. In any case, I do not feel this is the critical problem with the argument, as it seems rather plausible that something like this 'desire of recognition' is an important part of the emotional nature and behaviour of humans. There is also the problem of the 'epistemological status' of the stylised history of how the need for recognition asserts itself. Clearly, the story about the master and the slave is not empirical history. But it can be taken as a kind of compressed, essentialised history that highlights the essentials of the historical process on a very long time-scale, while not technically corresponding to real history in detail. 
The most critical part - especially viewed from 2024 - is more the claim that liberal-democratic society is the adequate and therefore in a sense _final_ form to satisfy the need for recognition. 

## Post-historical states: the end of imperialism

In Chapters 23-25 Fukuyama discusses inter-state relations and why a fatalistic view (basically, 'realism' in international relations) that sees inter-state competition and imperialism as a constant and unalterable condition is becoming progressively out-dated at the EoH. While he acknowledges the (partial) appropriateness of this view for previous periods where states were still primarily led by aristocratic-military elites pursuing grandeur and landgrabs, he argues that such states tend to disappear as market relations spread globally and states adapt and modernise, becoming bureaucratic machines aiming for economic efficiency. Of course, there are theories (eg. Marxism) that imperialism and war have a straightforward economic explanation and in fact flow from capitalist development. But putting aside the myriad of details whether late 19th century European imperialism in Asia and Africa were economically rational and whether the purportedly economic explanations of 'over-accumulation' are empirically sound (they are clearly not), there is a more basic problem that such theories cannot explain. Namely, wars *between* developed LDs have effectively never happened since such states emerged in the 18th-19th century. Wars happened betwen the mature capitalist states and late-industrialising challengers that were not LDs (Imperial and Nazi Germany, Imperial Japan, Tsarist and Communist Russia), but the archetypically LD states like the US, Canada, the UK or France never went to war with each other. From a purely mechanical application of the economic logic it would seem puzzling why the world's leading military power (the USA) never invaded its sparsely populated, militarily insignificant but very resource-rich Northern neighbour, Canada; yet not only did this never happen, but it seems even ridiculous as an idea. Fukuyama argues that this shows wars are not primarily about economic rationality, but rather 'thymos' or the drive for greatness, a motivation primarily characteristic of aristocratic-military elites condemned to extinction in market-based modern societies. 
It seems to me this explanation does not contradict a materialist (or 'economistic') explanation of the lack of war between mature LDs, but rather complements a deeper structural reason. In developed market societies economic competition is the supreme force: most energy of individuals goes into economic success, which permeates the entire culture and dominates the direction of social development by the sheer inertia of money. A capitalist society is rather un-warlike: instead of aristocratic warrior-lords who despise economic calculations, they are run by managers and businessmen who want more money and are populated by consumers who want more consumption. The elites are too rational and the general population too well-fed and self-indulgent to go to war, and they know that their neighbours in other LDs are the same. Beyond attitudes, war between mature LDs becomes economically irrational as well: landgrabs to acquire physical resources and population make ever less sense, as these are no longer the primary factors in economic success, overridden by technology and the labour force's skills level. 
This does not mean of course that there would be no wars. Violent nationalism and power politics operating with land grabs will continue to prevail among states that are not liberal democracies. War will also flare up at the meeting points of the 'post-historical' world of mature LDs and the 'historical' world of less economically developed and illiberal states, especially if the latter have some resources (like oil) that the mature LDs need. Yet, as the dominance of market relations spread globally the 'post-historical' region will also expand and render war more and more meaningless between these states. 

## The Last Man: souls at the end of history

I had imagined the tone of Fukuyama's book to be triumphalist, and I assume this is how most people imagine it as well. In fact, it is one of melancholy, especially its last part titled 'The Last Man', which I also found the most original and, frankly, the most moving. 
The Last Man is a figure who would appear at the end of history and raises the question if this condition is sustainable, and perhaps even more fundamentally, if it is desirable.  
If liberal democracy does triumph and has no potent enemies on a systemic level, would life in this 'final' form of society be truly satisfying for humans? Critiques of the claim that LD satisfies both desire and thymos come in two broad varieties. Left critiques assert that in fact recognition is not universal in LDs because important inequalities remain. While this is technically true, in the most mature LDs the remaining inequalities seem either rather trivial (and therefore manageable) or stemming variation between individuals and therefore necessary and likely to be ineradicable. Who will want to stage a revolution because of the remaining wealth inequality in Denmark, a matter of arithmetics? Of course the situation is much worse in other rich countries, but even in countries like the US or the UK political efforts by left populists to mobilise by stirring up moral indignation about inequality failed. All appearances suggest that people's blood is not boiled by the existence of millionares; indeed, those towards the bottom of the income distribution are more obsessed by money and consumption than anyone else and instead of wanting to overthrow the market system producing these outcomes, want to become millionaires themselves. Much of the remaining left today are more human rights liberals than socialists, constantly on the look for new rights and injustices and pushing for equality along these new dimensions. Whether these efforts are well-justified or not, this is hardly incompatible with LD and a market system, and instead entrenches it further, while in its extreme forms they seem self-defeating as they want to fight inequalities that simply come from biology. In either case, nor left egalitarianism nor the more 'cultural' (or 'woke') left seems capacious (or indeed willing) enough to disturb the stable (on a world historical scale) equilibrium of LD and market economics. 

A more fundamental issue is whether the recognition offered by mature, market-based LDs (EoH societies) is satisfying at all? Individuals in these affluent societies are all reason and desire: content with themselves and with a life of pursuing pleasures and economic success, all equal and perhaps equally banal too. All greatness comes from discontent, from a yearning to be different and in a sense recognised as superior to others. But in a perfectly homogeneous society of equal, rational consumers there is an equality of all and no great causes to die for. The ultimate virtue there is toleration: every lifestyle is equal and nothing is superior. Not only that, but the Last Man knows from history that there is no absolute value system or privileged perspective and perhaps former beliefs to the contrary were all folly:
> 'The last man at the end of history knows better than to risk his life for a cause because he recognizes that history was full of pointless battles in which men fought over whether they should be Christian or Muslim, Protestant or Catholic, German or French. The loyalties that drove men to desperate acts of courage and sacrifice were proven by subsequent history to be silly prejudices. Men with modern educations are content to sit at home, congratulating themselves on their broadmindedness and lack of fanaticism.'

While in pre-modern societies people were born into a belief system that formed a full horizon to live within, once this is lost, it is perhaps impossible to re-insert oneself into such a horizon. While some individuals in post-historical societies might rebel and try to completely attach themselves to a set of values, they are faced with the dilemma of being confronted a shopping list of value systems that they can arbitrarily choose from. Jaded by the pervasive relativism and materialism of their own society, they will have little prospect for success to find a way back to the sincerity of belief they yearn for.  
If humans are defined by the struggle for recognition and the struggle with nature for self-preservation and both of these are given to them automatically at the EoH, then in a sense they cease to be humans, and resemble more domesticated animals:
> 'The end of history would mean the end of wars and bloody revolutions. Agreeing on ends, men would have no large causes for which to fight. They would satisfy their needs through economic activity, but they would no longer have to risk their lives in battle. They would, in other words, become animals again, as they were before the bloody battle that began history. A dog is content to sleep in the sun all day provided he is fed, because he is not dissatisfied with what he is. He does not worry that other dogs are doing better than him, or that his career as a dog has stagnated, or that dogs are being oppressed in a distant part of the world. If man reaches a society in which he has succeeded in abolishing injustice, his life will come to resemble that of the dog.'

Writing in 1990, Fukuyama ruefully contemplates that if the heroic citizens of Ceaucesu's Romania or Tienenman square - at their most free and most human in 1989-90 - would succeed in their goals, they will ultimately build affluent liberal democracies, so they can have peaceful and comfortable lives, and with that they will also probably never be as free and heroic as in that moment of struggle.

Where does this all leave us? Was the whole 'human story' about achieving physical security and material plenty, and once there, will we necessarily be disappointed? Perhaps, more importantly, would the sheer banality of this final society, with little scope for pride and greatness, be likely to destabilise it, as people will revolt against a society so devoid of outlets for _thymos_, for the desire to be great and recognised as such by others?  
Looking at the issue more soberly, the above picture of a completely homogeneous and - in a philosophical though not arithmetic sense - equal society is too simplistic. 'Megalothymia', the striving for greatness, never disappear in democratic societies, but live on in sublimated forms. Individuals with above average ambitions can find outlets for these impulses: in business, in technological/scienfitic innovation, in politics or in forays into the 'historical' world (though even there with diminishing odds: joining Latin-American guerillas 50 years ago - in a more sedated form today in the form of NGO work in Africa), not to mention sports and the media. The need for differentiation, for 'supernormal' recognition can thus be satisfied in more placid forms in the post-historical society. Becoming a Tik-Tok star, developing a new AI chatbot (that the lonely masses can date), or new drugs to extend the lifespan over 90 might not have the heroism of aristocratic warriorship or religious asceticism of past societies, nor the beauty of their arts. But then - with the decline of historical education - individuals in post-historical societies will be progressively less aware that 'formerly, all the world was mad', so perhaps will not feel disappointed or insignificant at all. While it is possible to imagine 'a future nihilistic war against liberal democracy waged by those raised within its bosom', by all those who find such a society meaningless and therefore intolerable, the truth is they are likely to remain a small minority and even for them it might be just a phase; in contrast to pre-affluence societies that were intolerable (or at least unsatisfactory) for _most_ people. Therefore, while a relapse into 'history' is conceivable, the forces pushing in that direction are likely to be too weak, so post-historical society is likely to be a 'stable attractor', at least vis-a-vis the forces of a 'philosophical revolt' against its very banality. It is not that at the end of history reason, desire and _thymos_ are all completely satisfied: but each of them are satisfied to a larger extent than by any society before and _that_ can be sufficient for it to sustain itself, however depressing its horizon might be for a small minority. Stuff just happens, anything goes.

# Countervailing forces

Let us now examine how tenable these theses are, both on a general level and against the backdrop of the last 30 years since The EoH's publication. 

## The crisis of liberal democracy: who gets respect(ed)?

Often when people refer to Fukuyama's book since 2016 it is with Schadenfreude on how wrong it was, in view of the political turmoil even in the richest Western democracies since the Great Financial Crisis of 2008, and especially since the mid-2010s. Some authors claim that we can in fact now declare the 'end of end of history'. It seems to me this is a mischaracterisation of the thesis of EoH as what it claimed was not that there would be no political turbulence within LDs, not to mention less affluent states. In fact, there is explicit discussion in the book on how authoritarian challenges might appear in LDs:
> 'Thus, despite the apparent absence of systematic alternatives to liberal democracy at present, some new authoritarian alternatives, perhaps never before seen in history, may assert themselves in the future. These alternatives, if they arise, will be created by two distinct groups of people: those who, for cultural reasons, experience persistent economic failure despite efforts to make economic liberalism work, and those who are inordinately successful at the capitalist game.' p.235

The argument operated on another level: while such movements can - and likely will - emerge, they are unlikely to offer systematic alternatives to a market economy and liberal democracy. This is surely true of the rightwing populist movements mushrooming in Western Europe and North America in the last 10-20 years: they can be accused of many things, but not with wanting to overthrow the rule of private property or the market. Indeed, while their views and slogans are confused and heterogeneous, a recurring theme is that 'elites' have corrupted capitalism with excessive regulation and planning (enviromentalism and wokery), and what they promise is a return to a 'real' market economy, as it was in the good old times. While they might be protectionist in rhetoric, not much has happened on that front so far, though the second Trump administration might prove to be different; even in that case though tariffs are not socialism, it was a standard tool of capitalist states (indeed their main revenue) before the second world war.  
The relationship of rightwing populists to liberal democracy is more complicated. On the one hand they are clearly illiberal in some sense, though it is worth analysing that a bit deeper. On the other, they - almost always - claim to be leading a _democratic_ revolt of 'ordinary people' against elites, and popular sovereignty is their constant reference, however comical it sounds from some of the distinctively elite leaders of the populist right calling for a 'people's army' (eg. Farage in 2024 campaign). In that sense rightwing populists are self-avowed democrats, not philosophical enemies of democracy, although it is another question to what extent they will/would undermine pluralist democracy once in power (almost completely in the case of Hungary).
